Compound Interest for Beginners

Compound interest is interest paid on interest. It is the reason a small amount saved early can beat a larger amount saved late — and the reason debt grows so fast when it is left alone.

The formula

With a lump sum: A = P(1 + r/n)nt, where P is the starting amount, r the yearly rate, n how often interest is added per year, and t the years. With regular monthly deposits, each deposit starts compounding from the month it goes in — which is why monthly saving is so powerful.

A worked example

$1,000.00 at 10% a year, compounded monthly, becomes $1,104.71 after one year — $104.71 of growth on $1,000.00. That does not look dramatic. Time is what makes it dramatic: $100.00 a month at 7% grows to about $121,997 after 30 years, even though only $36,000 was ever paid in. The extra $85,997 is pure compounding.

The rule of 72

Divide 72 by your yearly rate and you get roughly how many years it takes money to double. At 6%, money doubles in about 12 years. At 9%, in about 8 years. At 12%, in about 6 years. It is a quick way to feel the power of a rate before you touch a calculator.

Why starting early wins

Two savers both put $200.00 a month in at 7%. One starts at 25 and stops at 35 (10 years, $24,000 paid in). The other starts at 35 and saves until 65 (30 years, $72,000 paid in). At 65 the early starter has roughly $280,968 — more than the late starter's $243,994 — despite paying in a third as much. The extra 10 years of compounding did the work.

Three rules that matter

  1. Start now, even small. Time beats amount.
  2. Never break the chain. Monthly deposits, automatic if possible.
  3. Watch the rate. A 2% difference over 30 years is not 2% — it is tens of thousands.
