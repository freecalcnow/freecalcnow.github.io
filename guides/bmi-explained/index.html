BMI Explained: What Your Number Means

BMI — body mass index — is the single most-used number in health. It is also one of the most misunderstood. Here is what it is, how it is calculated, and what it can and cannot tell you.

The formula

BMI = weight in kilograms ÷ (height in metres)². In imperial units: BMI = 703 × weight in pounds ÷ (height in inches)². That is all there is to it — one division, no blood test, no scan.

The categories

For adults, the World Health Organization bands are: under 18.5 underweight; 18.5–24.9 healthy weight; 25–29.9 overweight; 30 and above obese (30–34.9 class I, 35–39.9 class II, 40+ class III). These bands are population statistics — they describe risk across large groups, not a diagnosis for one person.

A worked example

A person who weighs 70 kg and is 1.75 m tall: BMI = 70 ÷ (1.75 × 1.75) = 70 ÷ 3.0625 = 22.9 — inside the healthy range. Their healthy-weight range at that height is roughly 56.7 kg to 76.3 kg (BMI 18.5 to 24.9).

What BMI gets wrong

BMI cannot tell muscle from fat. A muscular athlete can score "overweight" while carrying very little body fat, and an older adult can score "healthy" while carrying too much fat and too little muscle. It also ignores where fat sits — fat around the waist carries more risk than fat on the hips — and it was designed for populations, not individuals.

What to check alongside BMI

  1. Waist-to-height ratio. Keep your waist below half your height — it predicts cardiometabolic risk better than BMI alone.
  2. Waist circumference. Above about 102 cm (40 in) for men or 88 cm (35 in) for women, risk rises.
  3. Body fat percentage. A direct estimate of fat versus lean mass.
